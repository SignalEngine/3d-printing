# Mascot animation research — making a robot chat-mascot feel alive

## 1. Animation principles for alive idle/reaction loops

**Disney 12 principles, applied to idles** ([StudioBinder](https://www.studiobinder.com/blog/what-are-the-12-principles-of-animation/), [NYFA](https://www.nyfa.edu/student-resources/12-principles-of-animation/)):
- **Moving hold** — never a dead freeze-frame. Even "still" beats carry breathing, a blink, a micro weight shift. This is the single biggest fix for "looks locked up."
- **Anticipation** — a small counter-move before the main action (weight sinks slightly before a step, hand pulls back slightly before a point) makes motion read as intentional, not sudden.
- **Overlapping action / follow-through** — different body parts move at different rates and settle at different times (head arrives, then shoulder, then arm; a cape/cable keeps moving after the body stops). Locked-legs-plus-only-head-move is the classic failure of skipping this.
- **Secondary action** — a supporting gesture (weight rocks onto one hip while the head turns) that reinforces the main action without stealing focus.
- **Arcs** — natural motion curves, not straight linear interpolation; AI video models default to slightly-too-linear motion, so describe curved paths explicitly ("hand arcs up and over," not "hand moves up").
- **Timing & spacing** — uneven spacing (slow-in/slow-out) reads as organic; even spacing reads as mechanical/robotic (ironically useful for us in some places, harmful in others — see robot section).
- **Asymmetry** — a symmetric idle (both feet planted, both arms mirrored) reads as a rendered rig, not a creature. Weight on one leg, one shoulder slightly forward, head tilt.

**Game-dev idle cycle practice** ([MoCap Online](https://mocaponline.com/blogs/mocap-news/idle-animation-game-dev-guide), [MoCap Online loop guide](https://mocaponline.com/blogs/mocap-news/idle-animation-loop)):
- Base idle loops run **8–12 s** with built-in weight shift and breathing — matches our 5–10 s clip length well.
- A single loop becomes noticeably repetitive to a viewer after **~90 seconds** — for a chat widget playing one loop repeatedly, plan for **2–3 idle variants** cycled/randomized, not one loop on repeat.
- **Fidget layering**: studios blend short fidgets (weight shift, glance, finger tap) on top of/between base idle loops rather than making one long complex animation — keeps the character "never fully still" ([garagefarm.net](https://garagefarm.net/blog/idle-animation-tips-to-animate-your-characters)). For us: treat "idle" and "fidget" as separate short clips that get shuffled, not one 10 s clip trying to do everything.
- Breathing cycle (chest/shoulder rise-fall) is called out repeatedly as the base "alive" signal underneath everything else.

**Making a RIGID robot (no squash/stretch) feel alive** — inference + sourced pieces:
- **Overshoot and settle** substitutes for squash-and-stretch on a rigid body: a joint/panel moves slightly past its resting position, then eases back — "overshoot proves mass, settle expresses personality" ([Hackaday](https://hackaday.com/2021/09/03/smooth-servo-motion-for-lifelike-animatronics/)). Concretely: a shoulder plate that rotates to look at something should rotate a couple degrees past its stop, then ease back — not snap-stop.
- **Servo-style easing, not linear**: real actuated joints accelerate then decelerate (ease-in/ease-out) rather than moving at constant speed; constant-speed motion is what reads as "CG-stiff" ([best-microcontroller-projects.com](https://www.best-microcontroller-projects.com/arduino-servo-smoothing.html)). This is describable in a prompt as "eases in, holds, eases out" rather than "moves smoothly."
- **Mechanical secondary motion is the robot's version of hair/cloth physics**: filament spools keep gently spinning/settling after a stop, feed cables/tubes sway and lag behind arm motion, an antenna or LED-face element wobbles slightly on impact/turn — "hair, cloth, tails, belts trail the primary timing with proportional decay, each sway shorter than the last" ([Hackaday](https://hackaday.com/2022/05/11/animate-your-robot-in-blender/)). This is our best lever for "alive" without breaking the rigid-body premise: the RIGID parts move in servo-eased holds/overshoots, the LOOSE parts (spools, cable, tubing) do the flowing secondary motion.
- **Small randomness beats perfect repetition**: "add ease-in/ease-out, small pauses, slight randomness, avoid snapping to angles" to de-robotify motion generally ([search synthesis above], general animatronics practice) — but for OUR character apply this to organic-reading parts (head, torso weight) and keep true mechanical parts (nozzle, gantry) on a more literal, evenly-timed motion, since a real printer head genuinely does move in clean linear/orthogonal moves — that mechanical crispness is actually correct there and shouldn't be "humanized" the same way the body is.
- **Face/LED micro-expression**: since it's a screen, not a mesh, "micro-motion" = small changes — eye-shape squint, brief pixel flicker, blink-equivalent — not full expression swings. Ties directly to fix #5 below (LED smile too big): treat the face like a real device screen, changes should be subtle, screen-native (scanlines, brightness flicker) not cartoon-elastic.

## 2. Prompting image-to-video models for lively full-body motion

**Prompt structure (Kling-specific)**: two structures show up consistently across current guides —
- Simple: **Subject → Movement → Scene → Camera language → Lighting** ([Atlabs](https://www.atlabs.ai/blog/kling-3-0-prompting-guide-master-ai-video-generation))
- Fuller 2026 formula: **Camera movement + Scene setup + Subject action + Vibe/lighting + Time/audio** ([glbgpt](https://www.glbgpt.com/resources/kling-3-0-prompt-guide-for-better-ai-videos/))
- For stiffness specifically, a "sequential" method works better than one flat sentence: **Subject + Primary action + Environmental motion + Camera motion**, broken into beats rather than one generic verb (source flags that generic verbs like "moves" or "talks" are the main cause of stiffness) ([AtlasCloud](https://www.atlascloud.ai/blog/guides/mastering-kling-3.0-10-advanced-ai-video-prompts-for-realistic-human-motion)).
- **Describe motion as beats with timing**, not a single instruction — e.g. "first shifts weight onto right leg, then tilts head, then raises left hand" reads as a scripted sequence to the model rather than "gestures" as one blob.
- **Camera lock**: state camera behavior explicitly even when you want it static — "locked static camera, no pan, no zoom, no dolly" — guides consistently show camera language as its own required prompt segment; leaving it unstated is more likely to produce drift.
- **Leg/foot motion & weight shift**: per the physics-word research, name ground contact and weight explicitly — "weight settles onto left foot, right heel lifts slightly, subtle knee bend" — because I2V models struggle to invent weight physics the reference frame doesn't already imply. **The start frame itself should show a very slightly asymmetric, weight-bearing pose** (not a flat symmetric T-stance) since "if the starting frame depicts a character standing perfectly flat without compression or shadow contact, the engine struggles to invent that physics mid-stream" ([hailuoai.video](https://hailuoai.video/pages/knowledge/prompt-weight-impact-character-environment-interaction)). This is directly actionable for our keyframes.
- **Avoiding over-large facial changes**: dampen with explicit magnitude qualifiers — "subtle," "slight," "barely noticeable" — before every face/expression clause; per the research, these qualifiers "have a consistent dampening effect on variance" generally, so applying them specifically to face/LED clauses should curb the oversized-smile problem ([search synthesis], generalized from Vidu/Hailuo smoothness guides).
- **Negative prompts — keep short**: 3–7 targeted terms, not a long list; "extremely long negative prompts can actually make the animation look stiff... shorter and gentler wins" ([AtlasCloud](https://www.atlascloud.ai/blog/guides/mastering-kling-3.0-10-advanced-ai-video-prompts-for-realistic-human-motion), [Pollo AI negative prompt list](https://pollo.ai/hub/kling-ai-best-negative-prompts)). For us: `stiff pose, frozen legs, static lower body, morphing face, exaggerated smile, floating, foot sliding` — pick the 5-6 that map to OUR actual failure modes rather than a generic catch-all.
- **cfg_scale**: Kling exposes a 0–1 range, default ~0.5; lower = more creative/free motion, higher = stricter prompt adherence but can look more literal/stiff if the prompt itself is sparse ([fal.ai model docs](https://fal.ai/models/fal-ai/kling-video/v2.1/pro/image-to-video)). Given our stiffness problem is caused by under-described motion, not model disobedience, the fix is a richer beat-by-beat prompt at a **moderate cfg (~0.5)** rather than cranking cfg up — a high cfg on a thin prompt just enforces the thin (stiff) prompt more strictly.
- **5 s vs 10 s**: shorter clips (5 s) leave less room for the model's motion to drift/degrade and are easier to keep controlled for a tight fidget/reaction beat; 10 s gives room for a full anticipation→action→settle arc (e.g. "oops" or a presenting gesture) but increases drift risk — match clip length to the number of beats you're scripting (1 beat ≈ 5 s, 2-3 beats ≈ 10 s), not to what looks "safest."
- **End frames**: repeatedly recommended as a stabilizer — "when I add a last frame, even a slightly repositioned version of the same image, the motion between anchor points feels more deliberate" ([search synthesis]). For idle loops specifically, make the end frame a **near-copy of the start frame with a tiny pose delta** (not identical) so the loop reads as a settled moving-hold rather than snapping.
- **Multi-shot (Kling 3)**: supports sequencing up to ~6 shots in one generation with per-shot control over pacing ([Versely](https://www.versely.studio/blog/kling-3-complete-guide-features-prompting-workflows)) — worth using for the "short story" ask (anticipation shot → action shot → reaction shot) instead of stitching separate single-shot clips, since it keeps continuity of the mechanical secondary motion (spools, cables) across cuts.

**Three example prompts for an idle/fidget loop:**

1. *Weight-shift idle (5 s):* "Locked static camera, no pan, no zoom. Armored white-and-black humanoid robot stands facing camera at rest. Beat 1 (0-2s): weight slowly settles from center onto right leg, left knee softens slightly, torso tilts a few degrees toward the weight-bearing side. Beat 2 (2-4s): shoulders rise and fall once with a slow mechanical breath-like servo motion, filament spools at shoulder and hip continue spinning gently and decelerate. Beat 3 (4-5s): head tilts a few degrees, LED face shows a small subtle blink-flicker, holds. Subtle, slow, deliberate motion throughout. Negative prompt: frozen legs, stiff pose, symmetric static stance, exaggerated smile, morphing face, floating, extra limbs."

2. *Fidget/glance loop (5 s):* "Locked static camera. Robot in relaxed standing pose. Beat 1: head turns slightly to one side as if noticing something off-camera, small anticipatory shoulder rotation precedes the head turn. Beat 2: one hand's fingers flex/settle once, cable running from forearm to shoulder trails the motion and sways briefly before settling. Beat 3: head returns to center with a slight overshoot before easing to rest, LED face brightens subtly then dims to neutral. Slow, subtle, mechanical servo-like easing. Negative: static lower body, no weight shift, big exaggerated expression, jerky teleport-like movement, warped geometry."

3. *Thinking pose (8-10 s, two-beat):* "Locked camera. Robot shifts weight onto left leg while raising one hand slowly toward chin/chest height, arm moves with servo ease-in/ease-out and a slight overshoot at the top before settling. Filament spool at the raised arm's shoulder slows and wobbles very slightly on the stop. LED face shows a small, subtle 'thinking' animation (soft pulsing glow, not a large shape change), head tilts a few degrees. Torso remains slightly asymmetric throughout, weight never centered on both legs. Negative: symmetric idle stance, frozen legs, large facial expression swing, extra fingers, motion blur trail."

## 3. Real FDM printer motion, in prompt-usable detail

**What it actually looks like**, from printer-motion references ([FlashForge CoreXY vs bed-slinger](https://www.flashforge.com/blogs/news/corexy-vs-bed-slinger), [3Dnatives FDM guide](https://www.3dnatives.com/en/fused-deposition-modeling100420174/), [Forgely FDM guide](https://www.forgely3d.com/learn/fdm-printing)):
- **Perimeter/wall passes**: nozzle traces the outline of each layer — smooth, continuous, rounded-corner paths at a steady moderate speed, laying a single thin (~0.4mm) line of extruded plastic that's visibly deposited (slightly raised, matte) as it moves.
- **Infill (zigzag/raster)**: fast back-and-forth straight-line sweeps inside the perimeter, at a sharper angle (commonly 45°) than the walls, alternating direction each layer — visually this reads as a rapid, repetitive side-to-side "raster scan" motion, distinct in speed and geometry from the slower perimeter tracing.
- **Travel moves**: when the nozzle isn't extruding (moving between "islands" of a layer or retracting), it moves in fast, straight, direct point-to-point moves, typically lifted slightly ("Z-hop") to avoid dragging on the print — no material comes out during these, and they're visibly faster/sharper than print moves ([3Dnatives](https://www.3dnatives.com/en/fused-deposition-modeling100420174/)).
- **Layer change**: after finishing a layer's paths, the whole gantry (bed-slinger) or head (CoreXY) steps up by one layer height (typically 0.1-0.3mm) — a small, near-instant vertical increment, then the same X/Y path pattern repeats one layer higher.
- **Bed-slinger vs CoreXY**: on a **bed-slinger**, the bed itself slides back and forth in Y beneath a head that only moves in X/Z — so the visible motion is the WHOLE BED shifting, not just the nozzle. On **CoreXY** (matches a Bambu-style enclosed printer, which fits our chest-window "glass-fronted printer" description best), the bed is fixed and only moves in Z between layers, while the nozzle/head moves in both X and Y across the bed — this is almost certainly the better model for our chest window since the bed doesn't need to visibly slide in a small enclosed view ([FlashForge](https://www.flashforge.com/blogs/news/corexy-vs-bed-slinger), [Kingroon bed-slinger explainer](https://kingroon.com/blogs/3d-print-101/bed-slinger-3d-printer-all-you-need-to-know)).
- **Purge/wipe**: before or during a print, the nozzle often does a small dedicated purge line or wipe move off to one side/corner of the bed — a short, isolated squiggle or line distinct from the main print pattern.

**Realistic to get from a video model, or composite instead?**

My assessment (inference, not sourced — no source directly addresses "can Kling render accurate G-code-like motion"): **compositing is the better approach, ranked before regenerating more video-model attempts.** Reasoning:
- Video diffusion models have no concept of physical toolpath logic (raster angles, perimeter-then-infill ordering, retraction) — they're pattern-matching "small nozzle-like thing moves near colored blob," which is exactly the "blob/bubble" failure already observed. There's no reason to expect a generic video model to spontaneously produce correct zigzag/raster geometry; it has never seen labeled G-code motion as a distinct visual category in training data at meaningful scale.
- A **G-code toolpath preview render** (any slicer's built-in viewer — Bambu Studio, OrcaSlicer, PrusaSlicer all have one; or a standalone tool) can be recorded as a short looping animation showing genuinely correct perimeter/infill/travel motion, colored by feature type, and played at a chosen speed ([DeepWiki: BambuStudio G-code viewer](https://deepwiki.com/bambulab/BambuStudio/4.4-g-code-viewer), [DeepWiki: OrcaSlicer G-code viewer](https://deepwiki.com/SoftFever/OrcaSlicer/3.3-gcode-viewer-and-preview), [3DPEA gcode simulator](https://www.3dpea.com/en/gcode-simulator)). This is ground-truth-correct by construction and trivially loopable.
- Trade-off: a toolpath-preview render looks more like a **technical diagram/wireframe** (thin colored lines on a dark background) than a photoreal miniature printer — so it needs a stylistic pass (color grading, maybe a subtle bloom/glow, matching the chest window's glass-fronted aesthetic) to sit naturally inside the mascot's chest rather than looking like a bolted-on UI overlay. A real print timelapse would look more "real" but is harder to loop cleanly and to match to an arbitrary chest-window aspect ratio/framing.
- Recommended approach: composite a stylized, colorized G-code toolpath animation (or a simplified custom-rendered raster/perimeter loop matching the exact chest window dimensions) into the chest window in post, keep the AI-video generation focused entirely on the body/character motion, and never ask the video model to invent the print motion itself.

## 4. Top 8 concrete changes to our clip prompts (ranked)

1. **Fix the start keyframe pose, not just the prompt** — make every reference/start frame show a slightly asymmetric, weight-bearing stance (weight on one leg, slight torso tilt) instead of a flat symmetric stand. This is the single highest-leverage fix since I2V models struggle to invent weight physics the source frame doesn't already show ([hailuoai.video](https://hailuoai.video/pages/knowledge/prompt-weight-impact-character-environment-interaction)).
2. **Script motion as timed beats**, not one generic verb — "Beat 1 (0-2s): X. Beat 2 (2-4s): Y." Directly targets the "generic verbs cause stiffness" failure mode ([AtlasCloud](https://www.atlascloud.ai/blog/guides/mastering-kling-3.0-10-advanced-ai-video-prompts-for-realistic-human-motion)).
3. **Explicitly name leg/weight-shift motion every time**, even in "idle" prompts — "weight settles onto right leg, knee softens, torso tilts" — since leaving legs undescribed is why they're currently locked.
4. **Shorten and target the negative prompt** to 5-6 items matched to our actual failures (`frozen legs, stiff pose, symmetric static stance, exaggerated smile, morphing face`) rather than a long generic list — long negative prompts measurably increase stiffness ([AtlasCloud](https://www.atlascloud.ai/blog/guides/mastering-kling-3.0-10-advanced-ai-video-prompts-for-realistic-human-motion)).
5. **Take the printer chest-window entirely out of the video generation** — composite a colorized G-code toolpath render (from Bambu Studio/OrcaSlicer's viewer) in post instead of prompting for it. Removes the blob/bubble/wrong-nozzle-motion problem at the root rather than iterating prompts against it.
6. **Add explicit overshoot/settle and servo-easing language** for every held pose — "eases in, holds, settles with a slight overshoot" — to replace squash-and-stretch since the body is rigid.
7. **Route "alive" motion into the loose/mechanical secondary parts** (spools spinning down, cables swaying and lagging, panel/joint settle) instead of asking for exaggerated body motion the rigid rig can't sell — this is the correct substitute for squash-and-stretch on an armored character.
8. **Dampen the face/LED prompt clauses with magnitude qualifiers** ("subtle," "small," "barely noticeable") and describe the smile/expression change as a small shape delta on a screen (like a UI state change) rather than an open-ended "smile" — fixes the oversized-smile and likely also reduces morphing-face negative-prompt hits. Also add a hard negative-prompt term (`exaggerated smile`, `large facial expression swing`).
